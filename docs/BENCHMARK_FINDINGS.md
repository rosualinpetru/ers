# Benchmark Findings: Which Schemes Are Actually Effective

An evidence-backed comparison of the implemented range-search schemes, drawn
from the reports in [`benchmarks/`](../benchmarks). Every number below is
regenerated by `python3 scripts/gen_report_figures.py` (tables and figures in
`figures/report/`); explore the reports interactively with
`python3 -m ers.benchmark.plot.plot_benchmarks benchmarks`.

> **Reading older numbers.** Two measurement artefacts were fixed in v1.2.0
> (see `CHANGELOG.md`): the TDAG was built with duplicated cousin subtrees,
> which inflated the index size and result counts of every TDAG-based and
> interactive scheme by a domain-dependent factor, and the 3D dataset was
> loaded on float coordinates, which made the Linear scheme return nothing on
> it. All affected reports were re-run; the superseded ones are kept in
> `benchmarks/superseded/`. Token counts and precision of the old reports were
> correct; their index sizes were not.

## Method: which metrics, and why

Every scheme trades off three things, so we compare on exactly those:

| Metric | What it measures | Meaningful for |
|---|---|---|
| **trapdoor_count** | search tokens per query = the query-side leakage surface | all schemes |
| **precision** | true positives ÷ returned = the false-positive tax the client pays | lossy schemes only (`exact = False`); exact schemes are 1.0 by construction |
| **index_size** | server storage (a `sys.getsizeof` byte proxy, the same formula for every scheme) | all schemes |

**Timing metrics are omitted**: queries run in a process pool and several
sweeps ran side by side, so wall-clock numbers are contention-affected. The
three metrics above are deterministic. The workload is 20 queries per
(dataset, domain), two per selectivity bucket (query area 0–10 %, …, 90–100 %
of the domain), the same cached queries for every scheme.

## Findings

**1. Linearisation leaves the quadtree untouched and trades storage for
tokens on the range tree.** `quad_brc`/`quad_brc_hilbert` and
`quad_src`/`quad_src_hilbert` are byte-identical in every metric on every
dataset: the Hilbert quadrants *are* the curve's recursive construction (the
paper proves this). `range_brc_hilbert` is 3.3–4.3× smaller than `range_brc`
(a point lies in one binary-tree path instead of the product of two) and issues
2.9–5.2× more tokens, because a box shatters into several curve segments, each
with its own cover; the factor grows with the domain, as the query's *surface*
does.

**2. Single range covers minimise leakage and pay in precision; the TDAG
pays again in storage.** Every `*_src` scheme issues exactly one token.
`quad_src` returns 40–45 % false positives on average and almost nothing but
false positives for small boxes straddling a high-level cut (0.05 in the
smallest bucket at 2¹²). The TDAG's cousins lift this to 0.66–0.74 (`tdag_src`)
for 302–606 MB of index — 2.4–2.8× the range-tree product and 15–22× the
quadtree, since a point lies in two vertices per level of each attribute's TDAG
and the products multiply. The curve TDAG (`tdag_src_hilbert`) is one TDAG and
needs only 65–92 MB, but its single `[min, max]` interval contains every
excursion of the curve outside the box, so precision is lower (0.60–0.68).

**3. The packed R-trees are the single-round sweet spot.** `rtree` and
`hilbert_rtree` reach ~0.9 precision (0.97 for STR at 2¹⁴) with tens of tokens
and the smallest structured index (≤ 20.5 MB, below the quadtree's). Their
precision is flat across query sizes because the only false positives are the
outside points of boundary leaves (at most 3 per leaf with capacity 4), a
perimeter quantity. They dominate every single-cover tree on precision and
storage at once, for a modest token premium.

**4. Curve packing vs axis tiling: a draw, slightly favouring axis tiling.**
Hilbert packing issues fewer tokens (91 vs 141 at 2¹⁴); STR tiling has equal or
better precision that improves with the domain (0.97 vs 0.92). Axis-aligned
tiles fit axis-aligned boxes more tightly; the curve groups points into fewer,
more elongated leaves. The curve does little that a plain sort-and-tile does not.

**5. Interaction buys back precision at the lowest token count.**
`interactive_src_hilbert` issues 11 / 15 / 25 tokens in total (one in round one,
the rest in round two) for 0.78–0.81 precision — fewer tokens than any R-tree
and than exact `range_brc` (21 / 30 / 49) — and is exact with a BRC second
round. The native `interactive_src` (lexicographic document order) needs 17 /
32 / 61 tokens for 0.75–0.79: its surviving document indices form one run per
row of the box, whereas curve order leaves fewer, longer runs. Round one is in
both cases exactly `tdag_src`'s single token. The price: a round-trip, client
state (two TDAGs and the filter), and 100–150 MB of index (less than the
native TDAG the scheme improves on).

**6. Data-dependent dividers do not help axis-aligned workloads — and the
mechanism is now clear.** The default dividers balance *occupied cells*. On
cali every cell of each attribute's marginal is occupied, so the cell-balanced
cuts coincide with the uniform ones: `range_brc_data_dependent` and
`tdag_src_data_dependent` build *identical* trees to their uniform versions. On
spitz, whose marginals have gaps, a third of the vertices differ and the
numbers move by a few percent either way. On the curve domain, where only a
quarter of the cells are occupied, the cuts fall at arbitrary distances, the
dyadic alignment that keeps a segment's BRC logarithmic is lost, and the
tokens explode: `range_brc_hilbert_data_dependent` 200 / 661 / 2,872 vs 60 /
112 / 256 (up to 11×), `quad_brc_hilbert_data_dependent` 214 / 331 / 2,931 vs
77 / 147 / 335; no precision is gained, BRC being exact either way. The
mass-based curve TDAG (`tdag_src_hilbert_data_dependent`) is slightly *worse*
than the uniform one (0.58–0.62 vs 0.60–0.68) with a larger index.

**6b. Weighting mass by records (`*_data_dependent_records`) pays only for a
single cover over clustered records.** On spitz (28,837 records in 660 cells at
2¹⁴) `tdag_src_data_dependent_records` reaches precision 0.72 vs 0.58 (uniform)
and 0.61 (cells), for a 2.1× larger index (1.3 GB); on cali the gain is 0.01 and
on nh_64 (one record per cell) records = cells. Every exact scheme gets worse:
`range_brc` 49 → 75 tokens (cali), 49 → 68 (spitz); `quad_brc` 335 → 589 / 408;
the curve BRC trees improve over cell weighting (764 / 470 vs 2,872 / 2,931 on
cali) but stay 2–3× above uniform. The record-weighted TDAG also has far more
vertices (12–32× the domain size vs ≈4× for cells and 3× for uniform).

**7. Three dimensions amplify everything (nh_64, 2¹⁸ cells).** `linear`
issues 128,788 tokens per query; `quad_brc` 7,911 (an octree BRC of a box is
its surface in octants); `range_brc` only 126 but stores 228 MB for 4,096
points. The Hilbert range tree shows the 2D trade at full scale:
`range_brc_hilbert` stores 12.6 MB (18× less than the product) and issues
4,715 tokens (37× more), because a 3D box shatters into about a thousand curve
segments. `quad_src` holds one token at precision 0.53 and `tdag_src` 0.61 for
951 MB (`tdag_src_hilbert` 0.55 for 23 MB); the R-trees again land the best
lossy trade-off (182 / 202 tokens, precision 0.91 / 0.90, 4–5 MB). The
data-dependent curve trees degenerate to 58,221 and 58,946 tokens. Two of the
earlier "walls" were implementation artefacts: the curve range tree could not
answer a 3D query because the range cover expanded vertices disjoint from the
query (about 350 s per query; 0.3 s after pruning, same cover), and the deep
data-dependent curve TDAG only runs through the harness's sequential fallback
(its tree is too deep to ship to the process pool). Every (scheme, dataset)
cell now has completed query results.

## Recommendations by goal

- **Exact answers, storage is cheap:** `range_brc` (fewest tokens). Storage
  matters more than tokens: `range_brc_hilbert`.
- **Exact answers, storage is tight:** `quad_brc` — 8× smaller index, still
  exact.
- **Single round, false positives acceptable:** a packed R-tree (`rtree` for
  axis-aligned workloads, `hilbert_rtree` for fewer tokens) — ~0.9 precision
  at the smallest structured index. Prefer it to any single-cover tree unless
  one token per query is a hard requirement; then `tdag_src`, budgeting
  ~3× the range-tree product in storage.
- **Leakage-critical, a round-trip is acceptable:** `interactive_src_hilbert`
  (SRC round two for ~0.8 precision, BRC round two for exact).
- **Do not** reach for the data-dependent variants of exact schemes on
  axis-aligned range queries; they cost tokens without buying precision. The
  record-weighted TDAG is worth it only for single covers over records that
  pile into few cells, or for workloads whose queries follow the data.

## Bottom line

The space-filling curve earns its keep where its locality is used by something
else — the packing of an R-tree, the inter-round filter of the interactive
scheme — and not as a drop-in indexing domain: as such it shrinks the
range-tree index at the cost of the query's surface in tokens and leaves the
quadtree unchanged. The packed R-trees are the most effective single-round
schemes, the Hilbert double index the most token-frugal way to near-exact
answers, and data dependence a cost on this workload.
